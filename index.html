Mongolian coal
riding the rails
towards a brighter future

New railway lines to move exports are up and running in the Gobi region of the country, with further expansion plans expected to not only provide seamless connectivity to China, its neighbour and top coal customer, but also boost mining production.

In southeastern Mongolia, not far from the Khangi border checkpoint with China, a railway track ends just short of the fence, awaiting the day when a direct connection to the country’s prosperous southern neighbour becomes a reality.

But if everything goes according to plan, that day could come this year, with the track in Mongolia’s Dornogovi province linking to an extension on the other side of the border that will allow transshipment and seamless rail delivery to China’s Mandal port in the Inner Mongolia autonomous region, located less than 10km (6.2 miles) away. It will operate as part of the Khangi rail line built by Mongolian Trans Line, a subsidiary of Gobi Mining and Transport Group, which is a privately owned company providing support services for mining and heavy industries.

Construction of the Khangi rail line leading to Mongolia’s border with China has been completed and is now waiting for the connecting track from the Chinese side to be built, which will allow train-to-train transshipment.

“Based on the current level of bilateral engagement and institutional momentum, there is strong confidence that a formal decision will be reached in the near term,” says Baldorj Tsogzol, executive director of Mongolian Trans Line.

Mandal serves as the major gateway for delivering Mongolian coal, the country’s biggest export, to the Chinese city of Baotou, an integrated industrial powerhouse for large-scale steelmaking and electricity generation. It is also where rare earth from Bayan Obo – a nearby mining complex that accounts for over 40 per cent of the world’s known rare earth element reserves – is processed.

Rare earth elements are indispensable materials in the production of a vast range of hi-tech items, including electric vehicles, medical scanners and defense systems. Extracting and separating them is a highly energy‑ and heat‑intensive process that depends heavily on coal‑fired power.

About 90 per cent of Mongolia’s exported coal goes to China to meet the country’s varied demands. This includes high-quality metallurgical coal, also known as coking coal, which is essential for steelmaking because it acts as both a reducing agent and a reliable heat source.

The Khangi rail line to Mandal is one of the major infrastructure projects highlighted in the 2024-2028 Joint Government Action Plan mapped out between Mongolia and China, alongside two other cross-border railway projects: Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod and Shiveekhuren-Sekhee. Rail transport has been identified as crucial to improving cost and time efficiencies for trade between the two countries, with mined materials taking up much of the capacity for Mongolia’s southbound freight.

New rail lines providing cross-border links at the Khangi-Mandal, Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod and Shiveekhuren-Sekhee checkpoints promise to accelerate the growth of Mongolia’s exports, particularly coal heading south to China.

This ambition was further underlined last year in high-level talks between the two countries’ leaders. During a meeting last September with Chinese President Xi Jinping, Mongolian President Khurelsukh Ukhnaa announced a goal of raising bilateral trade to US$20 billion and said mining exports would be the key driver.

Long road ahead

During peak coal mining periods in Mongolia, thousands of trucks can be seen hauling loads to the Chinese border on a daily basis along clogged two-lane highways.

From Tavan Tolgoi – the country’s largest coal mining area by reserves, located in the southern province of Omnogovi – it can take more than 24 hours to travel the 280km highway route to the crowded border checkpoint of Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod, depending on road and weather conditions.

The coal then has to be offloaded at Mongolia’s Gashuunsukhait terminal to be picked up by trucks from China for the cross-border journey via Gantsmod. Factoring in customs procedures, it can take an additional two to four days because of bottlenecks.

Coal transport in Mongolia has long relied on trucking, which poses numerous challenges including limited road infrastructure and traffic congestion, hampering the country's export growth.
After making the long drive to the Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod border checkpoint, trucks loaded with coal heading to China still need to queue at customs to wait for clearance.

Independent ratings agencies have classified leading coking‑coal producers in Mongolia’s south Gobi region as having a competitive advantage on the global cost curve, thanks to shallow deposits and simple strip‑mining conditions located near the Chinese border. However, that lower-cost advantage is eroded when the coal has to crawl towards the border by truck.

The broader mining and quarrying sector accounted for 29 per cent of Mongolia’s GDP in 2023, and all signs point to continued growth. Last year, there were 314 active licenses for coal mining in Mongolia – the second-largest number by category, after gold – and coal mines occupied the largest share of mined area with nearly 795,000 hectares (2 million acres). That is about 7.2 times the area of Hong Kong or 11 times larger than Singapore.

In 2024, Mongolia exported a record 83.7 million tonnes of coal, surpassing the 80 million mark for the first time and earning US$8.6 billion in export revenue. This accounted for nearly two-thirds of the country’s total export value of US$13.2 billion for its six key mineral products.​

Enhanced railroad connectivity to reduce over-reliance on trucking is vital to helping the country cope with this industrial growth and unlock its full potential.

Fast track to
productivity

Standing in one of the three stockpile areas at a Tavan Tolgoi coalfield operated by state‑owned mining company Erdenes Tavantolgoi – Mongolia’s leading coal producer in terms of annual output – one cannot help but be awed by how everything runs like clockwork. Trucks, each towing up to four containers that can carry a total of 130 tonnes of coal, queue to be loaded up.

A coal processing plant in the Gobi region of southern Mongolia, which is run by Erdenes Tavantolgoi, a state-owned company that is the country’s largest coal mine operator.

The Tavan Tolgoi mine is located in the Gobi region of Mongolia, which is estimated to hold about 8.1 billion tonnes of coal in total, with 72 per cent of that being coking coal.

Erdenes Tavantolgoi is producing 30 million tonnes of coal there annually, but that amount remains far from its actual capacity. The company is hoping that the newest stockpile areas – which cost 315 billion Mongolian tugrik (US$88.5 million) to build – can help increase output via its train-docking facilities.

Erdenes Tavantolgoi excavators dig coal at one of the company’s coalfield operations at the Tavan Tolgoi mine.

“Rail will change everything. It reduces cost, saves time, and improves safety and environmental performance,” says Ch Altanbagana, director of the mining and technology department at Erdenes Tavantolgoi. “Once the railway is connected, we expect export capacity to rise from the current 30 to 35 million tonnes to as much as 60 to 70 million a year.”

Each train will be able to carry up to 100 containers, offering tremendous cost savings through economy of scale. It is also a much more reliable logistics solution that eliminates delays and concerns around road transport, especially during Mongolia’s harsh winters when driving can become treacherous.

“Coal is a bulk commodity with low value density. Transportation cost directly affects its competitiveness – that’s why logistics are critical,” Altanbagana says.

Currently, there is only one rail connection running through Mongolia into China: the Trans‑Mongolian Railway, which links to Russia’s Trans‑Siberian Railway at Ulan‑Ude in the north, and in the south to China’s national rail network at the Zamyn‑Uud-Erenhot border crossing.

The Soviet-built railway has been the backbone of Mongolia’s foreign trade, with the Mongolia-China section operating for the past 70 years. However, coal comprises just a minor portion of its freight, and its infrastructure is also nearing maximum capacity and becoming outdated.

Erdenes Tavantolgoi’s third, and newest, coal stockpile area is equipped with a train-loading facility.

In contrast, the new Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod and Khangi-Mandal lines were built with coal transport in mind. While the Mongolia-side sections that connect to Tavan Tolgoi are already operational, track extensions are still needed to make it possible for cargo to be transferred directly from Mongolia’s broad-gauge trains to China’s standard-gauge trains.

For now, trucks are being used as a stopgap solution, travelling between the last station of each rail line and the nearest customs checkpoint.

Short cut to
long-term growth

While the Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod cross‑border railway is backed by both Erdenes Tavantolgoi and the Chinese state-owned company China Energy, the Khangi line in Mongolia demonstrates the crucial role that private investments play.

In 2022, as the world was battling another wave of the Covid-19 pandemic, Mongolian Trans Line put its workforce into high gear constructing the Khangi railway. It was completed in just eight months and created the shortest route from Mongolia’s coalfields to the Chinese border.

The new Khangi line includes a link to the established Tavan Tolgoi-Zuunbayan railway, which allows trains to take a short cut to the Khangi-Mandal border checkpoint.

The new line, which went into service in February 2023, added a convergence point in Nomgon, Dornogovi province, to an existing railway between Tavan Tolgoi and Zuunbayan, the latter of which links to Sainshand, the provincial capital and a major hub on the Trans‑Mongolian Railway. That gives trains the option to take a 227km short cut to Khangi, allowing coal from Tavan Tolgoi to reach Mandal in less than 28 hours.

New rail lines have been built in Mongolia to facilitate more efficient transport of coal to the Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod and Khangi-Mandal border checkpoints en route to Baotou, a major industrial hub in China’s Inner Mongolia region.

At the rail junction point, the train switches are currently being operated manually, but upgrade work is under way to enable remote control in the future.

The Mongolian Trans Line team at Khangi station is responsible for inspecting incoming trains and their cargo, as well as track maintenance.

Quality maintenance is important due to Mongolia’s harsh climate, which can wear down the tracks. The line is divided into six sections of 35km each, with operations divided into two shifts per day that each have six workers. Supporting structures such as ballast layers and earthworks undergo routine inspections to ensure structural integrity.

In addition, Mongolian Trans Line workers are facilitating speedier customs clearance by conducting careful inspections at the Khangi station, ensuring that all cargo container serial numbers are correct to prevent hold-ups at the border crossing.

“Every day, about three rolling stocks – meaning three loaded trains – come into this yard, and about 100 trucks also come in,” says Khurelbat Maralmaa, field coordinator at Khangi Transshipment Terminal.

At the Khangi Transshipment Terminal, coal is transferred from trains to trucks for the cross-border journey into China. Once the rail extension from the Chinese side is in place, transshipment between trains will become an added option.

“Every now and then, there is a typo in the numbers. It gets fixed here, before it goes all the way to the border."

Mongolian Trans Line is also working to introduce automated identification for containers and vehicles. Paired with corresponding systems from partner coal mines, data exchange will be fully automated and capable of reducing human error by up to 98 per cent.

Already, cargo volumes handled at Khangi en route to Mandal have surged, reaching 8.5 million tonnes in 2024 compared with 3 million tonnes in 2022. Efficiency in this corridor is expected to rise sharply once the connecting railway in China is finished, with capacity of the full design projected to be 20 million to 25 million tonnes annually.

The new rail lines will not only serve major coal producers such as Erdenes Tavantolgoi, but they are also expected to connect smaller operators located near stations along the routes to form an integrated supply chain for Mongolian coal. One example is Tumen Ail Coal, which manages mines located about one hour away from Nomgon on the Trans-Mongolian Railway. The Khangi line short cut has made it possible for this operator’s thermal coal to reach Mandal port in less than 24 hours, rather than several days, which ensures timely deliveries to chemical factories in China.

“Looking ahead, once the cross-border railway projects at the Gashuunsukhait-Gantsmod, Khangi-Mandal and Shiveekhuren-Sekhee border crossings are completed, Mongolia’s coal exports could reach 120 to 140 million tonnes during the 2027-to-2030 period,” Mongolian Trans Line’s Tsogzol says.

Byambadavga Bayaraa, president of the Mongolian Coal Association, which represents 25 coal producers accounting for over 90 per cent of all coal produced in Mongolia, says: “We have worked closely with authorities to make sure that the coal is not stuck at the border. One of the objectives of the association is to export higher-quality and competitive coal at a lower cost, which depends on good infrastructure.

“Rail is the future of transporting these mining products to international markets. All of our member companies are eagerly waiting for the development of these railroads.”
